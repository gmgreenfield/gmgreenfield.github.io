If you read any blog from a software developer/engineer/programmer, etc., they'll have a post about where LLMs are taking us, trend-wise. I've never written about it, but I've noticed enough patterns to see some real patterns solidify.

For one, the academic study of software engineering (which of course isn't really engineering in the true sense) will largely be dedicated to the realm of large language models. It'll require fewer people; many working software engineers who work on CRUD-based programs and web apps will eventually be unable to keep their jobs. Many will transition into other adjacent jobs, and that's a sizeable amount of internal business applications. Think of it as the typewriter pools from the '60s or a computer operator in the early '80s.

For all of my professional career, I've been involved in working on CRUD apps; I'm not exempt from this. I've made peace with that. Which is why I'm focusing on C, C++, and Clojure. LLMs aren't great with the more functional languages (I know because I've asked). Much of my identity is based on being a software engineer.

The select few that work on compilers, low-level networking, kernels, drivers, AI models, etc., will still have abundant work. I doubt it'll happen in a decade (conservatively), but eventually it will be made clear to the general public.

There are definitely too many professional developers today, which brings me to the next point. If an LLM can keep track of variables and data structures more efficiently than a human, then why would they be writing software in Python-type scripting languages? Wouldn't you want to use at least a much more efficient language or even just assembly? It seems to me that focusing on that domain: C, C++, assembly, etc would be a much better use of time.

Of course, I could be completely wrong. I'll admit that, if anything, I'm slightly above average in intelligence; the same goes for programming. I'm not looking down on anyone that loses their job due to being unable to compete against these things. It's led me to a bit of an identity crisis, like many others have.

These LLMs are going to need various forms of hand-holding and assistance, as someone is going to need to take responsibility and feel the consequences of any defect. But being able to sit down in front of a text editor and write out a valid program that fulfills the goal is going to be a rare skill. Think of it this way: a future programmer will see the code being generated; they'll be able to scan it and understand the individual patterns and make sense of the syntax, "oh yeah, I could do that." But they won't be able to, I mean, a sizable number of professionals today wouldn't be able to be productive without autocomplete.

To be indispensable, professionals should be able to produce something meaningful in the same fashion as a '70s or '80s programmer would. In that time, it was taught and encouraged for developers to internally picture the patterns of program design in their heads and only then sketch something on paper; computing time was a sought-after resource.

That's my plan at least. I could be completely wrong, but it should rhyme with this at least.
